Australian Health & Longevity

We live longer, but are we living well?

An analysis of health expectancy, long-term chronic conditions, and demographic divergence over two decades.

The Longevity Paradox

Over the past two decades, advances in public health and medicine have consistently pushed the boundaries of human lifespan. Australians are living longer than ever before, yet a crucial question remains: are those added years lived in good health?

While average life expectancy continues to climb, healthy life expectancy - the span lived free from chronic disease or debilitating illness - has not kept pace. The result is an expanding gap in later life marked by chronic disease burden, multimorbidity, and notable divergence between women and men.

Where you live shapes what you live with

Health conditions do not distribute evenly across Australia. Variations in population age profiles, regional lifestyle factors, and healthcare access produce stark state-by-state differences.

Tasmania faces the highest relative health burden nationwide, exceeding national benchmarks across all ten monitored conditions, largely driven by an older median demographic. Conversely, the Northern Territory records lower rates across most chronic conditions due to its younger population, although kidney disease remains elevated.

Population volume meets local health burden

A high prevalence percentage does not always tell the whole healthcare story: resource allocation requires understanding both rates and raw caseloads.

Looking at total numbers, New South Wales and Victoria account for over 55% of all diagnosed Australians across every major condition due to their massive populations, even though their baseline rates often hover close to the national average.

In contrast, Tasmania records the nation's steepest chronic condition rates (40.6% report at least one condition vs 34.5% nationally), yet its total patient volume remains modest. Conversely, the Northern Territory has lower overall volume and rates for lifestyle aging diseases, but faces severe localized concentrations in conditions like kidney disease.

Mapping the global flow of chronic conditions

Australia’s chronic health profile reflects distinct global migration streams. This Sankey diagram illustrates how diagnosis volume flows from world birth regions (left) into ten major chronic health conditions (right).

Migrants from Southern and Eastern Europe and North-West Europe account for large, concentrated flows into arthritis, heart disease, and type 2 diabetes. Because these cohorts largely arrived during post-war migration waves, they represent an older demographic facing pronounced late-life morbidity.

Conversely, flows from South-East, North-East, and Southern/Central Asia remain comparatively thin despite large total populations in Australia, driven by younger average age profiles and immigration health requirements.

From diagnosis to disability: the daily care divide

Living longer with a health condition is only half the picture. The fundamental measure of quality of life is whether illness strips away everyday independence. In the Census, a "need for assistance" refers to individuals requiring daily help with core survival activities: self-care, mobility, or communication.

This Sankey diagram maps how chronic conditions (left) divide into daily independence (sage) versus profound disability (warm coral). Over 2.24 million chronic cases in Australia require core daily assistance.

While conditions like asthma and arthritis affect millions of people, their relative disability burden differs dramatically. For dementia, almost the entire diagnosis flow enters the care system (85.0%), followed closely by stroke (46.9%). In absolute caseload, however, arthritis (464,800 people) and mental health conditions (429,500 people) generate the highest bulk volume of Australians needing core daily support.

Did our shift away from smoking come with a trade-off?

Over the past two decades, Australia achieved one of the world's most successful tobacco control records. Age-standardised smoking among people aged 15 and over plunged from 24.5% in 2000 to 13.1% in 2022, cutting premature deaths from lung cancer and cardiovascular disease.

However, during the exact same 22-year window, adult obesity expanded steadily. In 2000, fewer than one in five Australian adults (19.5%) were living with obesity; by 2022, that number had surged to 30.2%.

The two trajectories crossed around 2004. Today, metabolic complications linked to high body mass - including type 2 diabetes, osteoarthritis, and heart disease - have replaced smoking as the primary chronic illness burden stretching Australia's healthspan gap.

Conclusion

So, are we living well?

Australians are living longer, but not always better. A baby born in 2021 can expect about 83 years of life, yet around 12.5 of those years are expected to be lived in poorer health, and that gap has grown since 2000.

The gap

More years, but not all of them healthy

12.5 years

Life expectancy rose 3.4 years since 2000, but healthy life expectancy rose only 2.5. The time spent in poorer health grew from 11.6 to 12.5 years. Women live longer than men, but spend more of that time unwell (13.8 vs 11.2 years).

Age

Conditions build up as we age

9 → 78 in 100

About 9 in 100 children report one of the ten conditions the Census asks about, compared with 78 in 100 people aged 85 and over. What leads also changes: asthma in childhood, mental health conditions from 15 to 54, arthritis from 55, and dementia jumps from 10th place to 3rd at 85 and over.

Who and where

The picture differs by sex and by place

40.6% vs 34.5%

Women report more arthritis at every age, and twice the rate of mental health conditions at ages 20 to 24. Men have more than twice the rate of heart disease at ages 55 to 64. Tasmania has the highest share of people with a condition (40.6%, against 34.5% nationally). These state rates are not adjusted for age, so states with older populations look sicker.

Daily life

For some conditions, independence is at stake

85%

85% of people with dementia and 47% of people with stroke need help with daily activities, compared with under 1% of people with no long-term condition. Arthritis and mental health conditions are so common that they account for the largest numbers needing help (about 465,000 and 430,000 people).

The road ahead

An older Australia is on the way

17% → 24%

People aged 65 and over are projected to grow from 17% to 24% of the population by 2050, and those aged 85 and over from 568,000 to about 1.6 million. Meanwhile, smoking fell from 24.5% to 13.1% of adults, but adult obesity rose from 19.5% to 30.2%.

Why it matters

Taken together, the charts point the same way. Living longer is a real achievement, but the extra years are not all healthy ones. The conditions that limit daily life build up most in the oldest age groups, which are the fastest-growing part of the population. Judging Australia's health by life expectancy alone hides this, so planning for care and support needs to focus on keeping people well for longer, not only on helping them live longer.

The question is not just how long we live, but how well.
